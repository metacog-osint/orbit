GEO belt

Every object in and near the geostationary belt, on a globe, from public orbital data: where each one is, where it has been, which arrived or left, which sit beside a satellite of another owner and which changed orbit. Positions are good to tens of kilometres; it shows proximity and behaviour, never intent.

    Names that state another position

    Operators often put the slot in a satellite's name ("Eutelsat 12 West G", "Eutelsat 36B", "Hot Bird 13C", "Hispasat 30W-6") and rename it when they move it, while catalogues may keep the old name. These are the active satellites with a name, from any source, that states a longitude more than 1° from where the satellite is. Most such names are former names; a name is marked current or former where TIC’s alias file records which. The last column gives a name that does fit, where one exists.

    Listed satellites on the move

    Satellites with a listed role (inspection or servicing) that are drifting along the belt: where the newest public element set puts them, when the move began, what lies ahead at the last measured rate, and where they stop once they brake. Arithmetic on public element sets, not a forecast, and no statement of where a satellite is going or why.

    Listed roles

    Only objects whose role published reporting supports are labelled, each with its source. An unlabelled object has no listed role, which says nothing about what it is. "Declared" means the role is the operator's or the state's own description; "attributed" means it is an assessment by the analysts, tracking firms or government named in the basis. The status is as of the date of the newest source behind it; "uncertain" means no recent report was found.

    How to read this page

    Read this first. Public elements place a satellite at GEO only to within tens of kilometres, so no separation under about 50 km is claimed, and two objects may be farther apart or closer than shown. Operators also place satellites close together on purpose, including across companies by agreement, and a servicing vehicle docked to its client shows as 0 km. A short list of satellites with a published inspection or servicing role is labelled, with sources; every other object is shown as having no listed role. Everything here describes behaviour, never intent.

    Method

    • Data. Orbit's GEO region (orbit/geo_region.py): one Space-Track GP query twice a day for every object with a mean motion of 0.9 to 1.1 revolutions a day and an eccentricity under 0.3, with every element set kept, and CelesTrak's active GEO group for the "Active" colour. Files on Orbit's public geo-2026 release. The history from July to mid-November 2026 was copied from Noisefloor, which ran this page until 18 Nov 2026.
    • Region. The protected zone is the IADC geosynchronous protected region: perigee and apogee within 200 km of 35,786 km altitude, and inclination 15° or less. The graveyard is everything above it. Each day's list is compared with the day before: newly catalogued (launched within the last year), back in the public catalogue, entered the zone, raised to the graveyard, lowered below the zone, left the zone, dropped out of the list, and, for payloads in the zone, no new public elements for over 7 days. A satellite missing from public data hasn't necessarily stopped working: some are simply not published.
    • Globe. Every object near GEO, placed with satellite.js (SGP4, the standard model for public orbital elements) in the Earth-fixed frame, so a satellite holding its slot hangs still over its longitude. Tracks show what the published elements said at each time: each element set is propagated only until the next one, so manoeuvres show where they happened. The dashed line is the next 48 hours predicted from the latest elements, which a manoeuvre would break. The line-of-sight circle is where the satellite stands above the chosen elevation: geometry, not a beam footprint. A hypothetical beam (in the panel, for one selected satellite) is a model: a circular main lobe whose gain falls as −3(ψ/ψ½)² dB with the angle ψ from boresight (the form ITU-R S.1528 gives after ITU-R S.672), with a beamwidth of about 70λ/D degrees for a dish of diameter D at wavelength λ. It shows where a beam of that size could reach; it is not the satellite's real coverage. Published footprints aren't drawn on this globe; the Footprints page draws a few satellites' filed and operator contours, each labelled published, computed or estimated, with credit. Collections such as SatBeams are not used. A footprint file you hold (KML or GeoJSON) can be drawn on the globe: it is read in your browser and never uploaded.
    • Globe tools. Look from a city or any point: each satellite's elevation, bearing and range from there (WGS84, sea level), and for a satellite holding its longitude, the next sun-outage seasons, worked out as on Noisefloor's Sun outages page (sun outages stay in Noisefloor, which owns signals). Sun and the Earth's shadow draws the sun's direction and the day–night line (a low-precision solar ephemeris, about 0.01°), and in red the part of the belt inside the Earth's shadow, modelled as a cylinder one Earth radius wide. Relative motion (select two) gives their separation over the history window and the next 48 hours. Closest objects over time (select one) finds the five objects of any owner or type that come nearest in the same span. Each comes with a CSV. All of it uses the same positions as the globe, so it carries the same error of tens of kilometres; an inclined satellite swings north and south each day, so its separation from a neighbour can change by hundreds of kilometres within hours.
    • RPO pairs and arrivals. The switch draws, in red, a line between the chaser and the target of each pair in Secure World Foundation's sheet whose two satellites are both near GEO today, and rings in blue every object with an arrival or departure in the last 30 days. Names in the sheet are SWF's, matched to catalogue numbers by Orbit's RPO lane (TIC's satellite catalog, GCAT, or a reviewed alias). The sheet records activities from 2005 on; a pair drawn far apart today may have been close then.
    • Belt board. The satellites in CelesTrak's active GEO group, each placed by SGP4 from its newest Space-Track element set. A satellite is drifting when it moves 0.1° a day or more along the belt; otherwise it is station-kept below 1° of inclination and inclined at or above it. Close pairs are two satellites of different owner codes within 100 km of each other at the time shown; pairs that share an owner code are counted, not listed (the code is often a country, and operators place their own satellites together on purpose), unless one has a listed role. Orbit changes use Orbit's rule, the same as its orbit-change lane: a change is marked when it is at least 8 robust deviations above the satellite's own usual change between element sets and at least 0.25 m/s, with at least 10 pairs of sets to set that usual change. Each is labelled from the drift and inclination before and after: station-keeping when the satellite holds its longitude on both sides, otherwise started drifting, stopped drifting, drift rate changed, or an inclination change of 0.02° or more on an inclined orbit. A satellite with too few public element sets is listed apart with its last two sets, not judged by that rule; a second rule that needs only two sets lists its changes separately (a drift change above 0.25 m/s, a start or stop of drifting, or a longitude off the path natural drift explains, with an allowance that grows with the gap). Noisefloor's board, which this replaces, compared daily CelesTrak snapshots with fixed thresholds; the comparison of the two on 18 Nov 2026 is in method.md.
    • Listed satellites on the move. For each satellite with a listed role that the belt board classes as drifting, from the same element sets: the newest set's longitude, drift and height above or below geostationary altitude, and its age; "now" is that set carried forward at its own rate and is always marked extrapolated. A newest set more than 3 days old gives no position and no dates. The start of a move is estimated from the drift rates, assuming the satellite sped up evenly from rest. Dates ahead come in two cases: at the last measured rate, and, with three sets whose rates rise in turn, still speeding up (to at most 4.5° a day). Neither includes braking: on Luch (Olymp) 2's three long moves of 2023-2025 braking took 10 to 12 days, so once a satellite brakes, later dates come late. A stop longitude is shown only when two projections in a row agree within 1°; on those moves such projections came 7.6 to 8.6 days before arrival and were off by 0.5 to 4.6°. The chart shows each current mover's element sets against time, the gaps with no public set, the estimated start, and the newest rate carried forward (dashed) past "now"; ticks on the dashed line are the station-kept satellites in the table. Rules and the Luch 2 record: method.md ("GEO relocation tracker") and luch2-relocations.md.
    • Listed satellites with no recent public set. For each satellite with a listed role, the newest element set in Orbit's files, from the same GEO runs: a satellite is listed when that set is 7 days old or more (the age the "no new public elements" event uses), with the set's time, its age, where it put the satellite, and the earlier gaps of 7 days or more in Orbit's sets. A set that ends such a gap is listed for 30 days after it first reached Orbit's files, with the set before it and where each put the satellite. "First in Orbit's files" is the day of the GEO run whose day file first carried the set. This is about the source: Space-Track's public data has no set for these satellites for days or weeks at a time, a satellite can move while none is published, and Orbit cannot tell why none was. The daily lead on Orbit's main page carries one line when a newest set passes 7 days old, or a set comes back after a gap, in the 24 hours before the run. Rules: method.md ("Gaps in public element sets").
    • Callouts. Automatic highlights of behaviour worth a look, from the last 14 days: C1 close to another owner's satellite (under 100 km on some hour of a day), C2 approaching (three daily falls, ending under 200 km), C3 parked beside another owner (stopped after a manoeuvre within 0.5° of a station-kept satellite of another owner), C4 heading for an occupied slot, C5 an orbit change by an object with a listed role, C6 arrived in the protected zone, C7 retired to the graveyard, C8 went quiet. Each rule was frozen in writing with a list of published cases before it was run, then scored on four months of history and seven older cases (the validation file). C3 to C7 show by default; C1, C2 and C8 stay off unless you switch them on. C5 uses Orbit's orbit-change rule (from 17 Nov 2026): a change between two element sets counts when it stands 8 robust deviations above the object's own noise and is at least 0.25 m/s at GEO. On the same history it found 6 of the 6 published cases C5 was scored on (C5 itself found 5); most of C5's extra marks were station-keeping, a drifting object and natural inclination drift. An object with fewer than 10 pairs of element sets is not judged, so a rarely published object's move can be missed.
    • Names. The globe shows Space-Track's name. Search and each object's card also use every other name TIC's satellite catalogue gives it (GCAT, CelesTrak, Wikidata, WMO OSCAR and reviewed aliases), and search ignores spacing and reads "West" and "East" as W and E, so "12WG" finds "Eutelsat 12 West G". A name states a position only in naming schemes that hold one: "<number> West" or "East" in any name, Eutelsat's short forms ("Eutelsat 36B" is 36° E, "Eutelsat 117W A" 117° W), Eutelsat's "Hot Bird 13" names (13° E) and Hispasat's "30W-6" (30° W). Names give the slot only to the whole degree: Eutelsat's "12 West" slot is at 12.5° W and "7 West A" at 7.3° W, so a name within 1° of the satellite counts as fitting. Series letters such as Intelsat's "37e" or Astra's "2E" are never read as positions. A stated position is compared with the newest public element set's longitude, on the card at the time shown and in the belt board's table at the time the page data was built (code: orbit/namepos.py).
    • Owners are CelesTrak's codes: CIS is Russia and the former Soviet states, PRC China, ITSO Intelsat, EUTE Eutelsat, SES SES.
