What changed in Earth orbit.

    The last 24 hours

      New to this? The terms used here
      TIP
      Tracking and Impact Prediction: United States Space Command's reentry predictions for objects about to come down, published on Space-Track.org. Each gives a predicted time and a window; after the reentry, a last message gives the time it came down.
      Window
      The span either side of a predicted reentry time. Inside it the object can come down anywhere along its path, which may circle the Earth several times.
      GCAT
      Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects, the public catalog Orbit reads for objects, owners, reentry and breakup dates.
      Orbit change
      A change in a satellite's orbit, usually from an engine burn, that Orbit finds by comparing successive element sets (the published numbers that describe an orbit at one moment). Orbit lists only changes above set sizes, in metres per second, for the satellites it watches.
      LEO, GEO
      Low Earth orbit, below about 2,000 km. Geostationary orbit, about 35,786 km above the equator, where a satellite keeps pace with the Earth's turn and stays over one longitude.
      Drift
      How far along the geostationary belt a satellite moves each day, in degrees of longitude. Under 0.1° a day it is holding its place. A satellite raised above geostationary altitude circles slower than the Earth turns and falls back to the west; one lowered below it runs ahead to the east. Operators use this to move a satellite to a new longitude.
      Notable
      An orbit change by a satellite with a listed role on Orbit's GEO page (each role cites published reporting) that is more than keeping its place: it started drifting, stopped, or changed its drift rate, by a quarter of a degree a day or more. It says what moved and how, from published element sets. It is not a warning and says nothing of why.
      Breakup
      An object that came apart in orbit, or collided with another, as GCAT records it.
      Debris, rocket stage
      Pieces left in orbit by launches and breakups, and the spent upper stages of rockets, catalogued alongside working satellites.
      MEO, HEO
      Medium Earth orbit, between low orbit and geostationary, where navigation satellites such as GPS fly; highly elliptical orbit, swinging far out and back close each turn.
      NORAD number, COSPAR ID
      An object's catalogue number from the US space surveillance catalogue (25544 is the International Space Station), and its international designator: launch year, launch number that year and a piece letter (1998-067A).
      Owner code
      A two-letter country code beside its flag (CN China, RU Russia, JP Japan). SU is the Soviet Union, which GCAT keeps apart from Russia. An intergovernmental owner shows its code only, such as ESA, the European Space Agency.
      RPO
      Rendezvous and proximity operations: one satellite manoeuvring close to another, as the Secure World Foundation's report records them.
      Element set, GP, SGP4
      The published numbers that describe an orbit at one moment (GP, general perturbations, is the current format), and the standard model that turns them into a position.
