Everything here is measured or reported by the sources named on it; Orbit adds no judgment.

Launches of the last 7 days at their GCAT launch site, scheduled launches at their pad, and the launch hazard and space-debris areas in navigational warnings now in force. Pale dots are every launch since 1 Jan 2024 at its pad, from Orbit's launch ledger (Launch Library 2), sized by count. A dotted line joins a scheduled launch's pad to a hazard area where the suite's Launch program finds the two coincide in time and place: a coincidence, not an attribution. Orbital launches, last 7 days

From GCAT's launch list. Payloads are those GCAT has cataloged so far; new ones are often added a day or more after launch.

Scheduled launches, next 14 days

From Launch Library 2. Scheduled times are the provider's or the press's and often move; some military launches are listed late or not at all. Countdowns run on your device's clock to the listed NET, refresh each minute, and are never finer than the NET's own precision; the status is Launch Library 2's when Orbit last read it. Headlines under a launch come from the Spaceflight News API and link to each outlet's own page; Orbit shows the headline only and has not checked the reporting.

Launch hazard and space-debris warnings in force

Navigational warnings that name rocket launching, space debris or reentry, from NGA's daily in-force memoranda (HYDROPAC, HYDROLANT, HYDROARC, NAVAREA IV and XII). NAVAREA XI (the Japan Coast Guard's area, including the waters off China) appears only where NGA rebroadcasts it. Windows and areas are the issuer's; the same area broadcast by more than one coordinator is one row. A scheduled launch is shown against a warning only when its NET falls in a window of four hours or less, or up to an hour before one opens. Under each, the suite's Launch program says whether the area also lies along a bearing and at a distance seen in that pad's closure history (time and place) or not (time only), with the distance, azimuth and the inclination the azimuth implies. Either way it is a coincidence, not an attribution. Not for navigation: mariners use the official broadcasts.

Predicted reentries

Source: Space-Track.org, United States Space Command (USSPACECOM), 18th Space Defense Squadron. Tracking and Impact Prediction (TIP) messages, redistributed under USSPACECOM's blanket approval for basic space situational awareness data, which requires this citation. These are USSPACECOM's predictions, quoted as issued; The Intelligence Company has not checked them. Not for operational use.

The newest TIP message for each object, from the last 5 days: its predicted reentry time and window (read as plus or minus that many minutes), and where the object would be at that time. That place is not where debris falls: inside a window of hours the object circles the Earth several times. "Track on the globe" draws the ground track it would follow through the whole window; it comes down somewhere along that line. Not on the flat map. A prediction whose whole window has closed stays for a day, marked closed; GCAT dates the reentry itself later. Names and owners are GCAT's where GCAT lists the object.

The last 30 days against the two years before

Counts from GCAT's own history: orbital launches by launching country and agency, payloads launched by owner, and reentries by owner, in the last 30 days and in each of the 24 windows of 30 days before them. "Above" or "below" means outside every one of those 24 windows. A count, not a warning. GCAT adds payloads and dates reentries as it learns of them, so the newest window for those two can still grow.

Orbit changes in watched satellites, last 30 days

Source: Space-Track.org, United States Space Command (USSPACECOM), 18th Space Defense Squadron. Element sets are USSPACECOM's, redistributed under its blanket approval for basic space situational awareness data, which requires this citation. The orbit changes listed here are The Intelligence Company's own analysis of those element sets. They are not USSPACECOM's, and USSPACECOM and Space-Track have not reviewed, endorsed or checked them.

A listed change means a watched object's published orbit moved by more than its element sets usually move. It is not a confirmed manoeuvre, a statement of intent or a warning: a re-fit after a tracking gap, a mislabelled element set or, in low orbit, storm-driven drag can do the same. Not for conjunction assessment, collision avoidance or any operational use. Satellite operators should use Space-Track's own services.

How Orbit marks a change: each pair of consecutive element sets is measured against that object's own usual set-to-set movement, after its steady drift is taken out. The change is given as the velocity it implies (along track, plane change, or orbit shape), which is a rough size, not a burn record. In geostationary orbit only changes of 0.25 m/s or more are listed. In low orbit, where plane and shape readings are noisiest, a change that is mainly a plane change or orbit shape is listed only at 1 m/s or more; each change also shows the velocity its semi-major-axis change alone implies. Changes less than a day apart are shown as one when they point the same way. A change measured across two days or more without an element set is labelled with that gap: it happened somewhere inside it. Quiet watched objects are counted, not named.

Reference · Anti-satellite tests in space, 1959 to date

Every anti-satellite test in Secure World Foundation's history sheet, as SWF compiled it: who tested, when, with which interceptor, against which target, and the cataloged debris created, since re-entered and still on orbit. A record of past tests, not new events; tests SWF adds are listed at the top after Orbit's next reading.

Reference · Rendezvous and proximity operations in space

Every robotic rendezvous and proximity operation in Secure World Foundation's history sheet, in SWF's three tabs and SWF's own words: who, when, which satellite approached which, and what for. Orbit reads SWF's dates into a span and names the rows whose dates it cannot read; satellites are matched to GCAT only where exactly one GCAT payload fits. A record of past and planned operations, not new events and not Orbit's judgment of intent.
